Andreas Almgren won New York on Sunday in his first marathon, in 2:06:01. In both frames we measured, his foot lands 0.45 leg lengths ahead of his hip. By the textbook, that's overstriding. At his speed, it's a long stride.

We ran the race photos through Flapp, our running-form app:

- **Elite runners land 0.18–0.45 leg lengths ahead of the hip.** Almgren is at the far end. For a 90 cm leg, 0.45 is about 40 cm.
- **Their shins stay within 13° of vertical, knee bent.** Every one of them.
- **Amateur reaches in our test photos land just as far ahead**, 0.42–0.51, but on a nearly straight leg with the shin tipped back 19–24°.

![Almgren at touchdown, two frames from two photographers: overstride 0.45 in both, shin −12.3° and −12.6°, knee 150° and 151°. Race photos.](/media/academy/nyc-2026-landing-ahead-of-the-hip/almgren-two-frames.webp)

## Same distance, different leg

"Land under your hips" works at an easy pace, not a fast one. Up to about 7 m/s you go faster mostly by lengthening the stride (Dorn 2012), so the foot lands further ahead. Almgren averaged 5.6 m/s.

Lieberman's study separates two things. The further the foot lands ahead of the **hip**, the more it brakes: Almgren pays that, like every fast runner. The further it lands ahead of the **knee**, the shin tipped back, the sharper the impact: that's the reach. A bent knee lets the thigh muscles take the landing over a few centimetres; a straight leg tipped back is a strut, and the load arrives at once. The authors' advice: land with "a relatively vertical tibia."

## Two frames, one number

Almgren's two frames come from different photographers at different spots and agree within a degree: overstride 0.448 and 0.445, shin −12.3° and −12.6°. Either he lands the same way every stride, or both caught the same step. Either way, two cameras gave one answer.

## Our band calls him "on the edge"

Flapp treats a shin within 10° of vertical as fine. Almgren (−12.3°, −12.6°) and Kiptum (−11.7°) come out amber. The band was set at everyday paces; at 5.6 m/s a little more lean may be normal. Eight elite frames aren't enough to move it.

## Heel or midfoot?

Flapp read one Almgren frame as midfoot and the other as heel, with knee, hip and shin matching within 1.5°. From a single frame, foot strike is a coin toss. Most marathoners at the 2017 World Championships landed heel first anyway (Hanley 2019). The heel isn't the problem. The shin might be.

## Check your own

Film or photograph yourself side-on at the instant your foot lands:

1. **Shin within ~10° of vertical, knee bent:** you land like the elites, however far ahead of the hip.
2. **Shin back 15° or more, knee nearly straight:** that's the reach. Try a slightly quicker cadence (see [overstriding](/academy/overstriding)).

## How we measured

Flapp's pose model finds the hip, knee, ankle, heel and toe. At touchdown it measures the hip-to-ankle distance in leg lengths, the shin's angle and the knee; a far landing leg is measured from its own hip. We used only side-on frames at the instant of landing. Chepkirui, Obiri and Kipruto were only in flight or at push-off in our photos, so they're out. Eleven frames: examples, not a study.
