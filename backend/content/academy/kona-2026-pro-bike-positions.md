**Updated 12 October 2026.** After the first version went out, readers pointed out that some of the pose model's points sat off the joints. They were right, so we checked every point on every photo and moved the ones that missed into the joint by hand. Six more race photos have come in since, and we added a position a reader asked about: Trevor Foley, from an earlier Kona. That makes twenty-one. And we replaced the "head position" reading, which another reader rightly called meaningless, with a measurement of how far the helmet rises above the back (section 4). All the numbers, figures and photos below are the corrected ones; what changed is listed under "How we measured".

After the Ironman World Championship we ran twenty professional bike positions, taken from race photographs, through the same pose model Flapp uses on your clips, plus one more that a reader sent in. The median pro rode with the torso 21° above horizontal, the upper arm at 87° to the torso, the elbow bent to 84° and the forearms tilted 25° up, with the top of the helmet 19% of a torso length above the top of the back. The forearms are the story: not one of the twenty-one rode them flat.

## 1. The hands have gone up

In every one of the twenty-one positions the forearms were tilted at least 19° above horizontal. Ten were past 25°, and three passed 30°. A few years ago a flat forearm was the default; at Kona nobody rode one.

Triathlon never had the rule that held road time trials back. Under UCI rules, the armrests in a road time trial could tilt no more than 15° until 1 January 2023, when the limit went up to 30°. Those rules do not apply to Ironman. Its 2025 rules limit how far the extensions reach, not past the leading edge of the front wheel, and set no limit on tilt. So the pros at Kona could go as steep as they liked, and the middle half of the field settled between 22° and 28°.

The aerodynamics back them. In a 2025 study that modelled one professional rider and checked the model in a wind tunnel, very high hands alone cut drag area by 3%. Combined with narrow elbows the saving grew to 7.7% for high hands and 11.2% for very high hands. Mat Steinmetz, a fitter who works with professional triathletes, describes the same change from the other side: 10–20° of bar tilt supports the upper body, relaxes the shoulders and makes a pocket for the head.

![Rider A, the flattest forearms in the field: 19°, with the elbow open to 91°. Flapp's overlay on the race photograph.](/media/academy/kona-2026/rider-a.webp)

![Rider B, one of the steeper ones: forearms at 28°, elbow closed to 78°, torso at 16°.](/media/academy/kona-2026/rider-b.webp)

Two cautions. The saving is rider-specific, and the same study found that a smaller frontal area did not automatically mean less drag. And the elbow pays for it: when the hands rise, the elbow tends to close. Across the field the two moved in opposite directions (r = −0.61). Three riders sat below the 75° elbow floor in our own range. That floor was written for flatter forearms; we have not lowered it because we have not yet found a fitter or a study that names a lower number. If you ride tilted extensions, read your elbow angle together with your forearm tilt, not on its own.

## 2. The torso is not as low as you would think

The median torso was 21°, and half the field sat between 19° and 25°. That is lower than most age-groupers ride, but it is not the flat back of a track pursuit. Steinmetz makes the same point: the pros' back angles have hardly changed in recent years. What changed is where the hands and the reach are.

There is a reason the back stops where it does. Fintelman's work on nineteen trained cyclists measured what a lower torso buys and what it costs. Going from 24° to 16° cut frontal area by about 5%, and going on to 8° cut about another 5%. But maximal power and every physiological variable fell as the torso dropped, and the flattest position was significantly worse than all the others. His models put the best torso angle around 17° for speeds between 32 and 39 km/h, and found that a fully flat back is never optimal.

Nine of the twenty-one positions were below 20°, the lowest at 13°. Pros can hold that: years of conditioning, four or more hours at a time. Most age-group athletes cannot hold that and still make their power. Flapp does not take points for a torso flatter than the range on aero bars, but it says plainly that this is the trade, and that it only pays if you can still breathe, push and hold it late in the race.

## 3. A closed shoulder is a choice, not an error

Most of the field sat close to the classic reference. The median upper arm was at 87° to the torso, and half the field sat between 86° and 91°. Fit guidance puts the reference at 90°: elbows under the shoulders. Fintelman's riders, in their own preferred positions, averaged 91° give or take 5°. Kona's median sits right on top of that.

Two positions closed the shoulder further, to 76° and 74°. The people who wrote the guidance never called that a fault. Dan Empfield's original description of the measurement says an angle below 90° is the athlete's personal choice, and that it is an angle opened past 90° that can become a problem.

![Rider C: the shoulder closed to 74°, forearms at 25°, elbow at 71°.](/media/academy/kona-2026/rider-c.webp)

![The same rider in Flapp's joint-angle table. The shoulder below its range reads "acceptable", amber, with no points lost. The elbow is flagged; see the caution in section 1.](/media/academy/kona-2026/rider-c-table.webp)

What to check if you ride like Rider C: whether the shoulders are shrugged up toward the ears, which is tiring and hard to breathe through, and whether the arms are carrying you or you are holding yourself up.

## 4. The head: how far the helmet rises above the back

The first version of this article showed a "head position" score of 100/100 on every rider, and a reader asked what that could possibly mean. Fairly: it checked whether the ear sat below a line through the hip and shoulder joints, and that line runs inside the body. The ear is at cheekbone height, so every aero rider passed, and the helmet, the part that actually sits in the wind, was never looked at.

So we measure the helmet instead. The pose model also finds the rider's outline. Through the highest point of the back we draw a level line, level because the wind is, and measure how far the top of the helmet rises above it, as a share of torso length. Where a helmet's tail lies flat on the back the outline cannot show where back ends and helmet begins, and on nine of the twenty-one photos the automatic back point landed on the helmet. We moved those by hand, the way the app now lets you move them on your own photo.

Every Kona pro had the helmet above the back: from 12% to 28% of a torso length, median 19%, half the field between 14% and 22%. Twenty of the twenty-one were at 23% or lower; one rode with her head held clearly higher, at 28%. That is normal. You have to see the road, and an aero helmet's tail is shaped to meet the back, not to hide under it. What it costs to go higher has been measured only as a comparison of two positions: in a CFD study of time-trial helmets, head up against head down changed drag by up to 6.4%, more than switching between three helmets did.

## What we did not measure, and why

No knee angles and no saddle verdicts. In a single race photo the crank is wherever it happened to be, so the knee angle is not a saddle-height reading. There is a second reason. Nineteen of the twenty-one photos were taken from the drive side, the side with the chainring, because most riders crossed the frame in the same direction past photographers on one side of the road. On two bikes of our own we measured that the drive side reads the knee at the bottom of the stroke 8–10° lower than it really is: the ankle point slides onto the dark chainring behind it. So we left the legs out rather than publish numbers we know are biased.

How we measured: twenty-two photographs from the 2026 race. One was rejected: the model had found the passenger on the camera motorbike instead of the rider, and Flapp returned no score rather than a wrong one. One more shows an age-group athlete and is kept out of these numbers; we are collecting those to compare with the pros. Position 18 is the one addition from outside the race: Trevor Foley, from a photo a reader shared in the comments. It comes from an earlier Kona, not the 2026 race, and we do not know who took it. On all fifteen, angles were measured in 2D on the side of the body nearest the camera, from four points: shoulder, elbow, wrist and hip. A pose model's points are its best guess at where each joint is, not the bony landmarks a fitter would mark by hand, and in the first version of this article several of them missed. So we went through all fifteen photos and moved every point that missed into the joint by hand, the same way the app lets you correct a point on your own photo. 57 of the 84 joint points moved, by a median of about 2 cm. One 2026 photo is a still from video, where the model had mixed up the joints; every point on it was placed by hand. On the rest the largest move was about 8 cm: a shoulder the model had put on the top edge of the back. Centimetres here are estimated from each rider's own hip-to-shoulder distance, taken as 50 cm. Every moved point is ringed on its photo. The helmet reading is taken from the rider's outline, not from these four points; its top-of-back point was moved by hand on nine photos, where the helmet's tail lay flat on the back.

Against the model's own points, the corrections moved the medians by 2° at most: torso 21° before and after, shoulder 86° to 87°, elbow 82° to 84°, forearm tilt 24° to 25°. They changed single riders more. Leaving out the video still the model misread, the flattest forearm went from 10° to 19°, and the number of shoulders closed below 80° went from six to two. Seventeen of the positions are men's and four are women's: too few women to compare the two fields yet, and we will when more photos come in. That is why we report the field rather than anyone's exact fit. Some athletes may appear in more than one photo; we could not check, so each photo counts as one position. The labels on the photographs are drawn by the same renderer the app uses on your own photos. It is one race, one day and one roadside viewpoint, so read these as the shape of a field, not as anyone's exact fit. We do not name the 2026 athletes: we cannot verify who is in every frame, and the point is the position, not the person. The one name here is the one a reader gave us with his photo.

## What to take for your own position

- **Try some forearm tilt before you chase a lower torso.** It is the trend with the best evidence and it is cheap to adjust. Every position here had at least 19°, and the middle half sat between 22° and 28°. Keep the elbows narrow; that is where most of the measured saving came from.
- **Do not copy a 15° torso.** Find the lowest angle you can hold for your race distance and still make your power, then test it in training at race effort.
- **A closed shoulder is fine** if you are not shrugging and you can breathe.
- **Keep the helmet within the pros' range.** Twenty of the twenty-one Kona pros kept the helmet within 23% of a torso length above the back. If yours rises more, drop the head a little, as far as you can while still seeing the road.
- **Measure yourself the same way.** Film from the side away from the chainring, camera at hip height, and include the bottom of the pedal stroke. If a point lands off a joint, drag it into place before you read the numbers. Then you can put your own numbers next to the table above.

## What we changed in Flapp

Measuring this field showed that three of our own rules were behind it, and we changed each one only as far as a source supports. The forearm-tilt range now runs to 30°. That is the ceiling road time trials allow under UCI rules; triathlon sets none, and one Kona position went past it, so read it as a reference, not a rule. A shoulder closed below the range on aero bars is amber and costs a third of the penalty, because no source calls it a fault. The aero estimate, which used to put every torso below 28° in one bucket, now separates three steps using Fintelman's measured frontal areas. And a torso flatter than the range costs no points, but comes with the concern you read above. The head reading that gave every rider 100/100 is gone. In its place the app measures the helmet above the back, lets you move both points it is measured between, and asks you to check the top-of-back point when the helmet's tail lies flat on it. A helmet up to 23% of a torso length above the back, where twenty of the twenty-one Kona pros sat, costs nothing; the one rider at 28% does not widen that for everyone. Above that it is amber with a small penalty, never red, because the one study we have compares two head positions and draws no line between them.
